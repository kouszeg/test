# The Pump on Broad Street

*How John Snow traced cholera to a single well*

At the end of August 1854, cholera struck Soho, a crowded district in the West End of London. It came with terrifying speed. In the first three days, 127 people living on or near Broad Street died. John Snow, a local physician, later wrote that within a few hundred yards of the corner where Cambridge Street meets Broad Street there were "upwards of five hundred fatal attacks of cholera in ten days."

Cholera kills through dehydration. The victim loses huge amounts of fluid through diarrhoea and vomiting, and a healthy adult can die within a day. In 1854 nobody knew what caused it.

## A bad smell

The accepted explanation was miasma: disease was believed to rise from rotting matter and travel through foul air. It was a reasonable idea for its time. Poor, filthy districts really did suffer most, and they really did smell terrible. The theory explained the pattern well enough, and it was the official view of the General Board of Health.

Snow disagreed. He was a respected doctor, known among other things for giving chloroform to Queen Victoria during the birth of Prince Leopold in 1853. Since the epidemic of 1849 he had argued that cholera was not breathed in but swallowed, and that it spread through water contaminated with the waste of the sick. If that was true, a cluster of deaths should point to a water source.

## Following the water

Most homes in Soho had no running water. People carried it from public street pumps. Snow obtained a list of the dead from the General Register Office and went from house to house. He found that nearly all the deaths had taken place within a short distance of one pump, on Broad Street.

The exceptions were even more convincing than the rule. There were only ten deaths in houses clearly nearer to another pump. In five of these, the families told him they always sent for water from Broad Street, because they preferred its taste.

A workhouse on Poland Street was almost surrounded by streets full of cholera, yet out of 535 inmates only five died. The workhouse had its own well and never sent anyone to Broad Street. A brewery stood a few steps from the pump, and none of its workers died. The owner, Mr Huggins, explained that the men had their own well, and in any case received a daily allowance of beer.

## The widow in Hampstead

The strangest case was far from Soho. A widow living in Hampstead died of cholera on the second of September, and her niece, visiting from Islington, died the following day. Neither had been near Soho for a long time. Snow went to Hampstead and spoke to the widow's son. She had once lived on Broad Street and liked the water so much that she had a bottle of it brought to her every day. The bottle delivered on the thirty-first of August had been drunk by both women.

## The handle

On the evening of the seventh of September, Snow presented his evidence to the Board of Guardians of St James's parish. They were not fully convinced, but they agreed to act. The next day, the handle of the Broad Street pump was removed.

This is often told as the moment the epidemic ended. Snow himself was more careful. By then, he wrote, the attacks had already fallen so far that it was impossible to say whether the well still contained the poison. Many people had fled the area, and many of those exposed had already fallen ill. Removing the handle may have prevented a second wave. It did not stop the first one.

## The first case

The vicar's assistant at St Luke's church, Henry Whitehead, set out to prove Snow wrong. He knew the parish and its people, and he believed the outbreak had other causes. The more he investigated, the more he found himself agreeing with Snow.

Whitehead discovered what was probably the first case. At 40 Broad Street, right next to the pump, a five-month-old baby, Frances Lewis, had fallen ill at the end of August. Her mother washed the baby's nappies and emptied the water into a cesspool in front of the house. When the cesspool was examined, its walls were found to be decayed, and it lay less than a metre from the well. Sewage had been seeping into the drinking water.

## The grand experiment

Soho was a single neighbourhood, and a sceptic could still blame its air. Snow's strongest evidence came from south London, where two private companies supplied water from the Thames. The Southwark and Vauxhall Company took its water from the river at Battersea, where the city's sewage flowed in. In 1852 the Lambeth Company had moved its intake upstream to Thames Ditton, above the reach of the tide and the sewers. Their pipes ran side by side down the same streets, so neighbours breathing the same air drank very different water.

Snow and an assistant visited the houses where people had died and found out which company supplied each one. The result was striking. Houses supplied by Southwark and Vauxhall had 315 cholera deaths for every ten thousand houses. Houses supplied by Lambeth had 37, which was even lower than the rest of London. Snow called it an experiment on a grand scale, with no choice by the people involved and no way for them to know which water they were drinking.

## Not convinced

The Board of Health reviewed the evidence and still concluded that the epidemic was caused by miasma. After the outbreak ended, the pump handle was put back. Accepting Snow's theory meant accepting that people had been drinking each other's waste, and few wanted to believe that.

In the same year, 1854, the Italian anatomist Filippo Pacini described comma-shaped bacteria in the intestines of cholera victims, but his work was almost ignored. The bacterium was identified again by Robert Koch in 1883 and 1884, and only then did germ theory slowly win. Snow did not see it. He died in 1858, at the age of forty-five.

London did change. After the "Great Stink" of 1858, when the Thames smelled so bad that Parliament could hardly sit, the city built a vast system of sewers that carried waste away from the drinking water. It was built largely to get rid of the smell. A last epidemic struck the East End in 1866, where the new system was not yet finished, and after that cholera never returned to London on that scale. The wrong theory had produced the right solution.

## What remains

Broad Street is now called Broadwick Street. A replica of the pump, without a handle, has stood there since 1992. Snow's map, with its small black bars stacked around the pump, is still shown to every student of epidemiology. Its lesson is not that one brave man was right. It is that he counted the exceptions and asked why they were different.
